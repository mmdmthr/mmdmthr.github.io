Our bottlenecks are understanding and remembering.

At least, those are mine and AI has changed how I deal with them.

It can read thousands of lines of code in seconds, identify patterns, and explain how an unfamiliar system works. It can recall syntax, suggest queries, and point me toward solutions without spending hours searching through documentation.

Tasks that once required hours of reading and searching can now take minutes.

But I don't think speed and memory are the most important parts of this equation.

AI can process vast amounts of information, but the quality of its output depends on the context it receives, the problem it is asked to solve, and how the result is evaluated.

This is where human judgment matters.

We bring experiences, knowledge, and perspectives that may not be explicitly present in the current context. We can connect seemingly unrelated ideas, question assumptions, and recognize when a technically correct solution doesn't make sense in the real world.

Of course, AI can do some of these things too. The difference isn't as simple as saying humans can think and AI cannot.

The point is that we shouldn't assume a convincing answer is necessarily a good one.

AI can produce a solution that makes sense within a given context while overlooking something important outside it. Without sufficient knowledge to evaluate the result, we might not even recognize what's missing. **The danger is not just getting a wrong answer, but lacking the knowledge to recognize it.**

Perhaps the goal isn't to compete with AI at what it does best. We will rarely win at speed or recall.

Instead, we should focus on developing the judgment to use AI effectively: questioning its assumptions, evaluating its output, and recognizing when a solution doesn't fit the bigger picture.

AI can help us understand more, remember less, and move faster.

But knowing more isn't the same as knowing what matters.
